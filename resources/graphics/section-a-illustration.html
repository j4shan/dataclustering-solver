On a rushed morning, you open a closet drawer for one garment and lift the whole stack. Today's outfit is a shirt, a scarf, and socks. You start at the item catalog — the inventory list on the door — and open every drawer it names, one after another. Each drawer comes out as a unit: you rummage through the stack, keep the piece that belongs to the outfit, and put every other garment back before moving on. Three drawers later you are dressed, but you have handled a much larger pile than the three things you're wearing. The useful effort is what you keep. The wasted effort is everything you lifted, picked through, and put back: neighbors that shared a drawer with something you needed. Tomorrow you'll reach for some of the same things, and the same drawerfuls will come out again. What if a smart organizer watched what you reach for, and learned? What if one morning you opened the item catalog and found the whole outfit in a drawer or two?

The same thing happens in a data warehouse. The closet is the warehouse; a drawer is a storage container; a garment is a record; the outfit request is a query; the item catalog is the index table. The lookup tells you which containers to open, and opening one activates the whole container: everything in it is materialized, and the records the query asked for are selected. The rest are records materialized but not selected — lifted, then put back. Both worlds pay for the whole containing unit; that shared cost stays fixed. The opportunity for improvement is the layout — the assignment strategy that decides which records share each container.
