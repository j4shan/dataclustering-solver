Picture the start of a busy morning at a dispensary. Three patients are waiting at the counter: one needs cough syrup, one needs eye drops, and one needs an antacid. The pharmacist checks the drawer chart on the medicine cabinet and finds each medicine box. But a small drawer cannot be opened one box at a time. The whole drawer comes to the counter, even when only one box in it is prescribed. Three boxes go to the patients; nine neighboring boxes are handled and put back unused. Six drawers stay shut. The chart avoids searching the whole cabinet, yet the pharmacist still handles twelve boxes to fulfill three prescriptions. Those nine extra boxes are the wasted effort.

The figure places technical names beside that familiar retrieval. In a data warehouse, the medicine cabinet is the corpus, a small drawer is a storage container, and each medicine box is a record. The counter batch is a query. The drawer chart acts as the index table: its lookup identifies containers that hold selected records, so the other containers can be skipped. Pulling a named drawer is container activation. Every record in an activated container is materialized, although only the prescribed records are selected. The remaining records are materialized but not selected — the warehouse version of boxes handled and put back. Both settings therefore pay for the whole containing unit. The open question is the assignment: which records should share a container? A restocking scheme can use demand history — past retrievals — to estimate how often each item is requested and rearrange equal-capacity drawers by demand band.

The right panel now states the formal problem: it defines these concepts, gives the objective formula for records materialized but not selected, and explains the system-wide data-skipping model and the effect of data clustering.
